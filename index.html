WastedSpace is a Roblox space sandbox game inspired by Waste of Space. "WoS" in this document always means the original game; everything else is the design for WastedSpace. Where a subsection describes WoS, it says so in its opening line. Temperatures are given in °C.

1. Universe Generation

WastedSpace's universe is procedurally generated through a hash tree: every place has a seed hashed from its parent's seed and its own part of the address (§1.1). Any place can therefore be generated by itself, without generating anything beside it, so the universe is generated on demand, never ahead of time.

1.1 Seed derivation tree

child seed = hash(parent seed, input)

The root seed is at the top. Every place below it is a node with its own seed, and so is every value of a place. A node's input is the part of the address it adds.

NodeDerived fromInput

One hash per level. Reaching any node takes one hash for each level above it, and its siblings are never computed: generating the region a player jumped to never requires generating its neighbors. That is what makes on-demand generation possible.

No two children share a seed. Every child of a parent has different inputs: coordinates are unique within their parent, the hash covers how many inputs there are, and a value is derived by its name, which can never equal a number or a coordinate.

One seed per value. Each value derives its own seed, so adding a value later changes no existing one.

Sector values. A sector holds at most one solar system (§2.1). Whether it has one, its resource richness and where its planets orbit are values of the sector, so its regions exist either way.

Surfaces belong to their orbit. A planet's surface is derived from its orbit region, so the surface is bound to the orbit that produced it.

Everything on a surface is a value. A surface's terrain value seeds every noise field that shapes its whole terrain, so the terrain is continuous across the surface. Its features value decides how many generated features it has and where they are, and each feature's seed is hash(features, i), where i is its index. That index is the feature's identity: it is what is saved when a clearable feature is cleared. Its flora value places its trees the same way, each tree's seed being hash(flora, i) (§5.3).

Wormholes are the exception. A region's wormhole is worked out from its own address, not from its seed (§3.5).

1.2 Nothing stored, nothing precomputed

Unmodified terrain, asteroids and space cost 0 bytes in DataStores until a player modifies them.

Nothing is precomputed. The tree reaches any node in one hash per level (§1.1), so generating on demand is cheap. A server keeps what it has derived in memory for its own lifetime (as with randmats, §9.5) — that is a cache, not storage, and it is gone when the server shuts down.

1.3 Server-only generation

The client never performs hash derivation and never holds seed values, raw or otherwise. The client works only in coordinate space (sectorX, sectorY, regionX, regionY, ...) and sends coordinates to the server for any action (travel requests, etc.). The server is the sole party that ever calls the hash/derivation functions and the sole party that generates terrain/scatter; it streams only rendered results (instances, voxel data) back to the client.

The derivation function is public in practice, but every hash also takes a 128-bit secret held only on the server, so knowing the function and seeing what the game generates is not enough to reproduce the universe. Hashes still never leave the server: the client is never a party to any hash value, which is also what lets the server decide what the starmap shows each player.

2. Map & Navigation

2.1 Coordinates

A location is addressed, not positioned: (sectorX, sectorY, regionX, regionY, surface) — universe X/Y, solar X/Y, and which surface, if any. It is written as a comma-separated string, for example 50, -50, 3, -7 or 50, -50, 3, -7, 2.

FieldMeaning
SectorA cell in the universe grid. Each sector holds at most one solar system; its system center is region (0,0) (§3.1).
RegionA cell in the sector's 21×21 subgrid, indexed −10..10 on X and Y. The odd size gives the star a center cell.
surfaceWhich place in a planet orbit region: 0 for the orbit itself, 1 for the planet's surface, 2, 3… for its moons' surfaces (§3.1). Optional: when left out, a Teleporter goes to the planet's surface and a HyperDrive arrives in orbit. Its main user is the Teleporter, which can send players to a teleporter in any of these regions of the same system coordinate, so the number says which one.

WoS's ±100 sector bound came from its packing math. The derivation tree makes the sector space effectively unbounded at negligible cost, so WastedSpace does not inherit it. The 21×21 subgrid stays because that is the design, not a limit.

2.2 Clustering

Systems cluster. Which sectors hold a system, and how rich each one is, is clumped rather than an even scatter: dense clusters of system sectors, with voids of deep space sectors between them. Sparse stretches give hyperjump distance meaning, since a HyperDrive's power cost grows with distance and with the mass of what it carries (§6.4).

Each sector samples a smooth noise field at its own coordinates to get a density, and the sector's seed rolls against that density to decide whether it holds a system. Because the field is smooth, neighboring sectors correlate without depending on each other. A sector with no system is still deep space, with its generated features (§3.4).

No crowding. A sector keeps its system only if at most two of its eight neighboring sectors also rolled one, so no system has more than two neighboring systems, and a cluster is a scatter of systems rather than a solid block of them. The check reads only the neighbors' own rolls, never their results, so sectors still don't depend on each other.

Richness follows density. Clusters are both crowded and rich; that is where players trade, compete and fight. Voids are not a destination to flock to — they are where players go to hide and build secret stations, away from everyone else.

Scale is set by jumps, not sectors — for example, crossing a void should take a large share of a mid-game ship's stored power (§6.4).

2.3 Starmap

The starmap shows only the area around the player. Seeing new space means jumping there, so each jump reveals the next stretch — the map is a tool for exploring, not a menu of every destination. The view is to scale with one grid square per sector, and its window is sized together with void size so a player in a void still sees something (§2.2). Solar mode shows the current system. Both show the view of each sector's summary and each region's header that the server builds for that player (§3.2). The starmap also shows heat signatures from heavy industry.

Heavy industry shows from a distance. Large reactors, crystal growth and other heavy industry give off a heat signature that shows on the starmap across their sector. A rich base in a cluster can then be found by anyone looking for a fight, and makes a better target than a starter planet.

Cloaking hides a region from the starmap and the telescope only, and not completely: a cloaked region shows only to a player within 3 regions of it. In a void, that makes a cloaked base close to impossible to find. Players can still travel to a cloaked region.

The advanced telescope is late-game tech that detects cloaked regions at sector scale. Within one sector of a cloaked region, it marks that sector on the starmap without showing which region; inside the sector, the 3-region range pins it down. Sweeping a void for one still takes many jumps, each costing power (§6.4).

The map edge glows faintly on the side with more systems beyond it — a direction to head in without revealing what is there. From a distance, a dense cluster is a brighter patch of sky.

3.2 Region data

The starmap, the telescope, jump cost, the wormhole test, arrival and the starter filter all need a few facts about regions nobody is in, and generating a whole region to read a couple of its properties would cost far more than the answer is worth. So a region's data comes in three levels, set by what each costs to work out and what needs it: the sector summary and the region header, which any server works out in under a millisecond, and the region body, built only by the server that hosts the region. Each level is derived from the one above it, from seeds and addresses alone, so none of them is ever stored (§1.2); a server keeps what it has derived in memory for its own lifetime.

A roll goes in the header; anything placed goes in the body. A value belongs to the header if a few hashes of a seed decide it, and to the body if working it out samples a noise field across the region or places anything in it. A header therefore costs the same whatever the region holds, so any server can read any region's header without generating the region, and the body's cost stays on the one server that hosts it.

The body is built around the header. Generation runs in one order: sector summary, region header, the body (terrain, scatter, deposits, flora, features), then the region's saved changes and flags (§7), and only then does the region report ready. Each body value derives its own seed by name (§1.1), so the order those steps run in never changes what they produce. The body places nothing inside the arrival area around EnterLocation, so the header can fix where arrivals come in before anything else exists.

Saved state is never part of a header. A header describes the region as generated. A cleared feature (§3.4), a depleted deposit (§8.1) and everything built there come from the region's save when it loads. The starter filter's generated tests (temperate, breathable, natural color set, no randmats) read only headers, so the spawn rotation can check a planet without loading it; whether it is depleted is the one test that needs its save.

A client sees a view, never a header. Summaries and headers stay on the server, since they hold seeds (§1.3). A client gets a view of them, built per player, holding only what can be seen from a distance: whether a sector has a system and what is at its center; and for a region, its address, kind and name, the center body, the planet's biome, colors, gravity, atmosphere, air, temperature and rings, an asteroid field, a persistent deep space feature (§3.4), a wormhole without its exit, and a beacon's signal (§3.6). What is found by going there stays out of it: resources, randmats, and a clearable feature and what it holds, so an ambush looks empty until the pirates jump in. Cloaking removes a region from the view as §2.3 sets out.

Cost. Measured in the standalone Luau interpreter with the current 32-bit Hash.luau: one hash takes about 0.7 µs, a sector's system test and center about 17 µs, a planet list about 0.5 ms, and the headers of all 441 regions of a sector about 3 ms; a starmap window of 61 × 61 sectors takes about 60 ms. The 53-bit hash with the server secret does several times the work per hash, and a Roblox server runs slower than a desktop, so in game these are estimated at up to ten times as much. That is still small beside generating a region's terrain, it is paid once per server, and a starmap window is worked out across several frames rather than in one.

Time of day comes from the clock. DayCycleIncrement sets the planet's day length and StartingTime its phase: the time of day at a fixed epoch shared by every server. Both are rolled from the planet's seed. Day length is one roll for every planet, whatever its biome: 5 minutes to 2 hours, usually about 20 minutes, so some planets cycle fast and some slowly. The current time is StartingTime plus the real time since the epoch, scaled by the day length, so any server works it out the same way, nothing is stored, and a planet is at night on arrival only as often as night's share of its day. A tidally locked planet keeps StartingTime as its fixed hour (§4.2). In WoS, planets roll a StartingTime but load between midnight and dawn almost every time.

TerrainConfig is almost entirely [min, max] ranges: Height, Roughness, Slopes, MountainDensity, RockDensity, Temperature, plus a TreeSettings block covering branch counts, splits, angles and trunk sizes. Life and Water are derived from Atmosphere and WaterLevel rather than rolled.

In WastedSpace the biome supplies weights for these values instead of hard limits, and the seed rolls each one (§4.2): most land in the usual range, and rare ones fall outside it.

Rings: RingStart 6000–12000, RingsEnd 6800–15000, RingsAmount 500–1250, RingsType (Ice or Stone). In WastedSpace these become weighted rolls around the same values, with the outer edge always beyond the inner one, and ice rings likelier the further a planet is from its star, where ice lasts.

3.3 Names

Every system has a name of its own. A name is an invented word of three syllables, usually followed by a Greek letter or a title: Drogichas Xi, Crendethar, Ovaldoral Alpha, Chekigas Haven. No two systems within 8,191 sectors of the universe center share a name; beyond that, a name returns only after 268 million sectors.

Planets are numbered and moons lettered. A planet takes its system's name and a Roman numeral, counted outward from the center: Drogichas Xi IV. A gas giant's moons add a letter, starting at b: Drogichas Xi IV b. A rogue planet at a system's center carries the system's name alone.

Other regions in a system's sector show the system's name. Deep space regions have none and show their address.

open a filter for names that happen to spell an offensive word. Every sector's name is unique, so a blocked name needs a replacement rule, not a skip.

3.4 Deep space

Deep space is every region of a sector with no system: the voids between clusters (§2.2). There is no star. Solar panels have nothing to catch, and the ambient is cold vacuum, the slowest place to shed heat (§14.3), so a base out here runs on stored or generated power and real heat engineering.

Voids are where players hide and build secret stations, and long trips across them need stepping stones. Every deep space feature serves one of those: supplies, cover, or a prize worth the trip.

A Deep Space region holds at most one feature, rolled from its seed and weighted like every other roll (§4.2), or nothing at all. Whether it also holds a wormhole is a separate test (§3.5). What matters most to a player is whether a feature lasts.

3.5 Wormholes

A wormhole is a persistent link, the same for every player, joining two regions far apart, and each end is generated only when its own region loads.

Passing through. Flying into a wormhole takes you to its other end, the same way flying into a planet takes you to its surface (§3.1). It costs nothing to use, so it is a free route over a long distance. Passing through is a transfer like any other move between regions, and the ship arrives beside the exit wormhole (§6).

Blocks. For wormholes, space is divided into blocks of 64 × 64 sectors. Each block has one spot: a single region in the middle half of the block, picked from the block's own coordinates. Only the spot can hold the block's wormhole end, so a block holds at most one end, and two ends are always at least 32 sectors apart.

Pairing. Every block has exactly one partner block, worked out from its own coordinates by a Feistel cipher, also called a Feistel network (the structure behind block ciphers such as DES). Blocks are divided into nine interleaved layers by position (every third block across and every third block down). Each layer has a reach, and is cut into groups of its blocks sized for that reach, each group with its own key derived from the root seed. A block turns its place in its group into a unique number with the cipher (six rounds, twelve for the smallest groups), maps that number n to a far-off one with the hinge (N − 1 − n) xor 1, and runs the cipher backward. Doing the same from the partner leads straight back, so a block's partner's partner is always itself, no block is its own partner, and no two blocks share one. Each layer's groups are shifted by a different amount, so no part of space is cut off from wormholes passing through.

Which pairs are wormholes. Only Deep Space regions hold wormholes. A pair becomes a wormhole when its spots are within its layer's reach, a roll for the pair comes in under the layer's threshold, and both spots are Deep Space. Each layer's threshold is set so every layer holds about the same share of ends, so wormholes run from short hops to long jumps in about the proportions of the table: 2 : 3 : 3 : 1. Both ends test the same pair, so they always agree, and every wormhole goes both ways. A spot that holds a wormhole skips its usual kind roll; every other region rolls its kind as normal.

No region needs another. The test uses only the region's own coordinates, keys derived from the root seed, fixed numbers, and whether the partner spot's sector has a system. No other region is generated, nothing is read or stored, and load order does not matter. The regions' own seeds are not involved, so the seed tree is unaffected (§1.1).

open pair thresholds: they set how rare wormholes are. The preview uses about 1 block in 4 holding an end, so a starmap window of 10 × 10 sectors shows one about once in 160 windows (§2.3).

3.6 Event regions

discussed, not settled

Event regions hold content that is not generated from the hash tree: PvE encounters, seasonal events, or a beacon signal that leads to a one-off encounter. They live entirely outside the tree. Generation never reads them and they never feed into it, so an event can be added or switched off without changing anything else in the universe.

Beacon signals show up on the starmap and the telescope (§2.3). Following one jumps into an event instance, a separate place outside the universe grid, reached with a normal hyperjump, ship and all (§6). While a beacon is active, any number of players can follow it into the same event, and they can fight each other there. Each event sets whether players can damage each other inside it, so an event can be PvE only.

Players can build freely inside an event instance, but the instance is discarded when the event ends: only what leaves attached to a ship, and what players carry, comes back with them (§6.3). Every instance has an end time, so none becomes a permanent safe haven.

Events also come to players. An event can appear inside a real region as a temporary overlay that the region's own server rolls for, from its seed and the current time: a meteor shower, a derelict, a raid. Only regions someone is in run events, and an event ends with its region's server. It spawns clear of planets, hazards and builds by the same check arrivals use (§6.2). Event entities belong to no one and are never saved. Loot moved into a bin, a part welded to a ship, or a part a player locks becomes the player's at that moment. Whatever is left when the event ends is removed, and the region itself never changes.

Events are scripted. Every event, in a region or in an instance, is written against an Event API, the way Microcontroller scripts are written against theirs. Its methods place prefabs and ship blueprints at an exact spot or a safe one; pace the event with phases, timers and waits; react when a player enters or leaves, an entity is destroyed or an item is taken; announce, set starmap beacons and markers; fill containers from loot tables; and end the event. The API only creates entities that belong to no one, cleans them up when the event ends, keeps each event within a part budget, and has no access to saves or generation, so no event script can break either. Community members can write events too; the developers choose which ones ship with game updates.

3.7 Stars and black holes

A star heats everything in its own region, more steeply the closer it gets, and radiation rises the same way (§13.7), far more around a neutron star. Parts hotter than their material's heat tolerance take damage until they are destroyed, so flying into a star destroys an ordinary ship. The effect stays within the star's own region: from anywhere else a star is only visible, and the heat it gives the rest of its system is what makes inner planets hot (§4.1).

A rare randmat can change that. A hull whose heat tolerance holds survives inside, and powered cooling or a randmat that deletes heat (§14.2) lets a ship stay for as long as it can keep up. Surviving a star is a capability earned by the find.

A black hole destroys anything that crosses its event horizon.

4. Planets

4.1 Planet placement

Two noise fields derived from the sector's seed — a coarse field for broad material clumping, a fine field for local variation — are sampled along the system's orbital radius and combined. A hash-derived threshold cuts the field; local maxima above it become planets, with a minimum separation preventing adjacent peaks merging.

A planet's orbital radius feeds its biome selection weighting, so placement and climate come from the same value.

Measured across 400 systems: planet counts range 1–8, peaking at 4–5. Some systems are crowded, others nearly empty.

4.2 Biomes

Every planet has a biome — its type: Tundra, Desert, Ocean and so on. Biomes are authored, not generated. Each is a designed package: terrain profile, caves, material set, flora, resource table, hazards, skybox, ambient audio.

The hash does two things with them.

Selection. Which biome a planet gets, weighted by orbital position so systems read coherently — hot biomes inward, cold outward. Weighting shifts probability rather than locking the outcome, so outliers occur.

Variation. Every property a biome covers is a weighted roll: the biome sets the weights, and the planet's seed rolls each value during generation. A Tundra planet's seed sets how mountainous it is, how deep the permafrost runs, how much rock is exposed, its gravity, its resource richness, its atmosphere and air, and its ambient temperature, corrosion and radiation (§13.3). It cannot make the planet stop being Tundra.

Weights make a result usual, not certain. A biome's defining traits, listed in the biome table, are always present; beyond those, no outcome is ruled out, so somewhere in the endless universe there is a breathable Glacial world or a Barren world with air, however unlikely.

A number rolls on a range. A biome gives each number a low, a usual and a high value. Nine rolls in ten land between low and high, most often near usual, and usual need not sit in the middle, so a biome can lean one way. The tenth roll lands outside, half on each side, and each step further out is ten times rarer than the last, a step being the width from usual to that edge. Terra's gravity might run 0.8 to 1.2 of standard, usually 1.0: then 1 Terra in 20 is below 0.8, 1 in 200 below 0.6 and 1 in 2,000 below 0.4. Physical limits, such as gravity above zero, are the only hard edges.

A choice rolls on weights. Atmosphere, air and other choices are weighted lists, and every option keeps at least a small share of the odds. The only options left out are those physics rules out.

Later values follow earlier ones only where physics says so. A planet with no atmosphere has no air to breathe, and one too cold for liquid water has no oceans. Every value still rolls from its own seed, so the order values are worked out in changes nothing.

A planet's type is fixed at generation and never recomputed.

Tidal locking is a trait, not a biome. Any biome can roll it. Its chance follows the planet's distance from its star: likely in the innermost orbits, where a star's pull slows a planet's spin until one side always faces it, and rare further out. A tidally locked planet has no day cycle (§13.3): its sun stays fixed at a time of day set by the planet's seed.

There is no starter biome. WoS had an EarthLike type that existed only to supply spawn planets: limited resources, no randmats, always temperate, natural colors. That is the same mistake as putting RobotDepot in the planet type enum (§7). Starter planets are ordinary Terra planets that pass a filter — temperate (a character takes no temperature damage there at any hour, §16.4), breathable, natural color set, no randmats, not depleted (§8.1) — and the new-player spawn rotation picks from those. The filter only selects. Starter status never changes how a planet generates, so the rotation can change later without regenerating anything.

Gas giants have no surface. The surface region is open cloud with nothing to stand on. Gases and liquids can be harvested from the atmosphere into pipe networks (§15), which gives a reason to go there. Deep below lies a core of rock and metal, under pressure and heat no ordinary ship survives; a hull that holds, a rare randmat find as with stars (§3.7), can dive to it.

Only gas giants have moons. A gas giant has none, one or two, each a surface region beside the giant's own (§2.1), reached from the same orbit. A moon gives gas harvesting solid ground for its base, refinery and tanks. A moon rolls its biome from the outer ones (Barren, Tundra, Glacial), and is small and low-gravity. A moon of a gas giant in a cold outer orbit can roll tidally heated: the giant's pull heats its interior, so its geothermal gradient is as steep as a Volcanic world's (§13.8) under a frozen surface, a warm place to dig in a cold system. Other planets have no moons; their orbit and surface already give them all a moon would.

4.4 Planet colors

Each planet has its own colors. A region's server colors each terrain material from its planet's color set. Roblox sets terrain colors per server, and every region has its own server, so neighboring planets can look different.

A planet's colors are rolled as hue, saturation and brightness. Its PrimaryColor is rolled from its own seed. Hue is free in every biome: any biome can come in any hue, so a rust-red Glacial or a teal Desert is as likely as any other. The biome sets only the character of its colors, how bright and how washed-out they run: Glacial pale and bright, Volcanic dark, Barren dusty and muted. Saturation is capped on every planet, so none is neon magenta or electric blue. SecondaryColor follows the first by a weighted rule: usually a nearby hue, sometimes a contrasting one, rarely the full opposite. Each terrain material starts from its own base color and is shifted toward the planet's pair, so grass, rock and sand stay recognizable while the whole planet shares one palette, and grass can be maroon on one world and gold on another. A natural color set is one in earth tones, greens, browns and tans, which is what the starter filter checks (§4.2). Exotic's colors spread widest within the cap, for its odd colors.

A planet can have color patches. A few islands or stretches of its ground are a look-alike material, such as LeafyGrass beside Grass, colored from the planet's palette in a hue of their own. Roblox gives each material one color per server, so a planet has only as many patch colors as it has look-alike materials to spare, two or three at most. The terrain module's blend mask places them (§5.2). Trees take their colors from the ground they stand on, so a patch's trees match it (§5.3).

In WoS a planet's color comes from the sum of its coordinates' absolute values, so mirrored coordinates, and every coordinate on the same ring around the origin, share a color, and the palette is small.

5. Planet Surfaces

5.1 Surface generation

A planet's surface is 3,072 studs on a side: 48 save chunks (§7) and 12 cave squares (below).

The whole surface is generated when its region loads, and every player sees all of it.

  • A ship entering from orbit arrives high above the center (§6.2) and sees the whole landscape below: mountain ranges, crater basins, distant outposts and bases.
  • Terrain far from any player still blocks sight and stops shots. Sensors reach 1,000 to 2,000 studs, and artillery and railguns fire across valleys (§21).
  • Bases, extractors and parked ships far from the only player on the planet still stand on their ground and keep their sealed caves.

Each biome's terrain is a module written for it. A shared engine asks the planet's biome module about every column of the surface: how high the ground is and what layers lie under it. A module can also shape a band of height in full 3D, for overhangs, arches or giant fungal caps, and it sets its caves' character. The engine does the rest for every biome: exposed cliff faces, caves, and writing the terrain. Adding a biome means writing its module.

The planet's seed rolls the module's settings. Each setting rolls on a weighted curve, such as mesa height, number of terrace steps or how far canyons wind. The surface's terrain value does the rolling (§1.1), so two Desert planets have the same kinds of shapes in different proportions, and an unusual one is rare, never impossible.

5.2 Terrain shape

Ground comes in layers. Each biome sets its layers from the surface down, each with its own depth, so a dig, a crater or a cliff face shows them. A temperate planet might run grass, soil, rock, then basalt. Where the slope is steep, the top layer gives way to the biome's cliff material, so cliffs show bare rock.

Material sets the silhouette. Each terrain material is drawn with its own shape: snow and sand round off, rock, basalt and salt break into facets, and wood planks render as blocks. Collision is the same for all of them. A biome's materials are chosen for their silhouette as much as their color.

Biomes are built from a shared set of shapes: warped hills, terraces, cracked plates, craters, billowing drifts, sharp ridges, and blends between two sub-terrains within one biome. Examples include salt flats giving way to badlands, or frozen lakes to ice spires.

Each biome has its own shapes:

5.3 Tree generation

Trees are built from parts, not terrain. Each tree is a model generated from a tree type and a seed.

The biome sets the tree types; the seed decides how they look. Each biome names several tree types of its own, so they differ as much as the biomes do: giant broadleaf and conifer trees over shrubs on a Forest, ribbed cacti and broad rosette succulents on a Desert. A type holds a weighted range for every attribute of the tree: trunk height and thickness, how it branches and bends, its leaves and fronds, and their colors. The planet's seed rolls each type into one concrete style, and each tree's own seed adds a small variation on top. Trees on one planet match each other without being clones, and one Forest planet's conifers can be narrow spires with dark blue-green needles while another's spread wide with olive ones. Leaf and bark colors come from the planet's palette and match the ground a tree stands on (§4.4).

Attributes that belong together roll together. A type links attributes such as trunk thickness to height and canopy size, so no roll gives a thin trunk under a huge canopy.

Each biome has a parts-per-tree budget. Branching depth, branch count and leaves per tip set how many parts a tree takes, and the budget caps those rolls. A biome of many small trees keeps each one cheap; a sparse biome can roll fuller ones.

A Forest grows giants under a closed canopy. Its trunks rise like walls and its crowns meet overhead, so the ground can't be seen from the air at all. Below, the trunks wall off pockets and hollows that are hard to find even on foot, which makes a Forest the planet for a hidden base.

Each tree comes from the seed. The surface's flora value places its trees, and each tree's seed is hash(flora, i), where i is its index, so a felled tree is saved by its index like any other change (§7).

5.4 Caves

Caves are winding tunnels. A tunnel wanders for several hundred studs, curving, climbing and dipping. By default it is 16 to 24 studs wide with a flat floor, and in places widens into a flat-floored room. Floors are mostly rock, with mud where a tunnel dips and water would collect.

Caves come with the biome. The biome weights how much cave its planets have, and each planet's seed rolls it. Most biomes have modest caves; extensive networks appear only where they make sense, such as crystal planets or worlds whose surface is extremely hostile.

Each biome sets its caves' character. It sets how wide tunnels run, how often they open into rooms, and how much mud collects in dips.

Tunnels stay at least 40 studs down, except at cave mouths. Some stretches of surface are mouth zones, and there a tunnel starts at the surface and slopes down.

Tunnels come from the seed, square by square. The surface is divided into 256-stud squares, and each holds up to two tunnels from its own seed. Building any piece of ground needs only the tunnels of the squares around it, so nothing about caves is stored (§1.2).

5.5 Abandoned mines

Mines are dug, caves are grown. A cave is part of the terrain. An abandoned mine is a generated feature, placed by the surface's features value like any other (§1.1). Its generator carves its corridors and sets its supports after the terrain and caves are built, so a mine cuts through any cave it meets.

A mine is straight corridors. They branch at right angles, with shafts down to lower levels, mud floors in places, and loot near rich deposits. Corridors are 24 × 24 studs, framed every 24 studs by WoodPlanks supports set into the terrain: two posts and a beam, leaving 16 studs between the posts and 20 under the beam.

5.6 Modern and alien facilities

Not every structure is a ruin. Mines are old timber workings; facilities are recent, or not human at all. They are generated features, placed by the surface's features value like mines (§1.1).

Modern facilities are military-grade. They sit underground behind shells of faceted Concrete, with hard edges and flat planes that read as built, never natural.

Alien sites are black geometry. Faceted dark bodies form tapered arches, slanted walls and doorways whose sides lean inward, lit only by sparse lines of light. Tall towers rise from them, crowned with large alien weapons (§20.2).

A facility is captured like any NPC site (§20.3). Its shell is terrain and belongs to no one, but its fittings are parts: machinery, power, lights and the sealing door, all in one lock group held by a claim controller. Once the controller is destroyed or drained, players lock the parts they keep and the rest is gone when the region unloads. A cleared facility is a sealed space ready-made underground, since rock counts as wall (§13.8): replace the door broken on the way in and it is a base.

A facility carves its own space. Its generator runs after the terrain and caves are built: it clears the rooms, pours the Concrete shell around them, then lines and furnishes them, all in one pass. When the site is cleared, the rooms and shell remain (§20.3).

Behind the concrete is a lining of parts. A facility's rooms are finished the way players finish a dug tunnel (§12): wall, floor and ceiling parts set against the rock. Its shell can be dug like any unclaimed ground, so tunneling in is always possible, but it only reaches the lining, which has to be breached like a hull. Modern facilities are lined with armor such as titanium. Alien sites are lined with a material so tough that only late-game weapons break it.

Every alien site has a hidden way in. Somewhere in its shell is a weak point, such as a buried vent or a sealed hatch, that players find by digging. A hacking tool opens it, so a player without late-game weapons can still get inside.

6. Travel Between Regions

Every move from one region to another is a transfer, and every transfer works the same way: the ship and its passengers leave one server and are rebuilt on another. Only what starts it differs:

TriggerWhat starts it
HyperjumpTriggering a powered HyperDrive (§6.4).
Entering a planetFlying into the planet in its orbit region, which takes the ship to its surface (§3.1).
Leaving a surfaceFlying up out of a planet's surface region, which takes the ship to its orbit.
WormholeFlying into a wormhole, which takes the ship to its other end (§3.5).

6.1 One server per region

One server hosts one region for its whole lifetime. Servers aren't assigned regions in advance: a server becomes a region when it starts and is told which one it is. Instancing keeps positions within float32 precision, removes seams between loaded chunks, and gives each region its own part and physics budget, which is what lets large asteroids and ships run. The cost is that everything crossing a transfer has to be carried explicitly, and every transfer is a loading screen.

A transfer leaves the current server. If the destination is already running because someone is there, the player joins that server; otherwise a new one is started for it. Either way the player reconnects, and their character and ship are rebuilt on arrival (§6.2).

Fix: anchored rebuild. Every part spawns anchored, and joints are rebuilt from the saved ship data, never from which faces happen to be touching. Then the whole ship unanchors in one frame, and network ownership is set in that same frame (Roblox doesn't allow setting ownership of anchored parts).

Fix: seat directly. While the ship is still anchored, arrival calls Seat:Sit(humanoid), which welds the player in without any contact, so gravity, thrust, gyros and orientation stop mattering. Every passenger is reseated, not just the pilot: on departure each occupied seat's index is saved with the ship, and on arrival each player goes back into that seat. The ship stays anchored until the passengers who arrived together are seated. A passenger whose teleport had to be retried is seated in their recorded seat whenever they arrive, since Sit needs no contact even on a moving ship.

Fix: a checked arrival point. A region's EnterLocation, and a planet surface's (§3.2), marks the center of an arrival area, not an exact spot. Each arriving ship is placed at an offset from it, and its whole bounding box is checked first against triggers (a planet, a wormhole), hazards (a star up close, a black hole's horizon) and anything already built or floating there. If the check fails, another offset is tried. Arriving ships therefore never land on top of each other, and since the ship is still anchored while it is placed, nothing it touches can fire before it is released. 6.3 Carrying the ship

What travels. Everything joined to the traveling assembly (the HyperDrive's on a hyperjump, otherwise whatever touched the trigger), including anything joined through a CJoint such as a towed ship, plus the contents of its containers, and every seated player. Loose items and standing players are left behind, as in WoS.

Nothing anchored. If the assembly is joined to anything anchored, the transfer fails, the same as when the ship itself is anchored. WoS instead carries anchored parts joined by a CJoint, lifting a whole base into orbit with the ship.

Joints travel with their state. Each hinge, piston, rail and CJoint is saved with the ship: which two parts it joins, its settings, and its current position (a hinge's angle, a piston's extension, where a part sits along a rail). The rebuild restores each one at that position, so moving parts arrive exactly where they left. WoS may not do this, which could explain its joint and rail failures (§6.2).

A ship has one owner at a time. Each ship is its own saved record, owned by either a region or a transfer in progress, never by a player aboard it; passengers' records only point to it. Only the owner may build it, and handing it over is a single atomic change of owner:

  1. Departure. The origin server saves the ship as in transit on this transfer, then removes it from its world before teleporting. From then on the record is the only copy.
  2. Arrival. When the first passenger arrives, the destination claims the record, which succeeds only if it is still in transit on this transfer, and builds the ship only after that.
  3. Failed teleport. A passenger whose teleport fails is retried. The origin takes the ship back, with the same claim, only if every passenger failed. A passenger who never gets through stays in the origin region, like a player left standing. Only one claim can succeed, so the ship is built exactly once.
  4. Both servers crash. The record stays in transit. Every passenger's record notes the transfer, so whichever of them next joins is sent on to the destination, and the ship is claimed there.

No combination of failures leaves two ships or none.

Parked ships use the same record. A ship left in a region is owned by that region. The region's manifest lists only which ships are there; the ship record is the truth. On load, a region skips any listed ship whose record names a different owner, so a crash can't leave a ship in two places.

Inventory travels the same way. A player's tools, worn items and inventory are held in their player record and handed over by the same claim, so nothing goes missing on arrival.

The player record holds everything that is the player's own, not the world's:

PartWhat it holds
ItemsInventory, tools, and worn apparel with the parts attached to it (§16.4).
LocationThe region the player is in, and if seated, the ship and seat; or the transfer in progress, so a player who rejoins is sent on to where the ship went.
BodyHealth, air tank level, and radiation dose with the time it was saved. Dose fades as a clock while the player is offline (§13.7), so leaving the game is no faster than waiting it out.
KnowledgeRandmat scans (§9.2), and later plant gene scans.

What the player owns elsewhere is not copied into it. Ships are their own records, and faction membership is held by the faction (§19).

6.4 Jump cost

Only a hyperjump costs power. Entering a planet, leaving a surface and passing through a wormhole are free.

A jump's power cost grows with distance and with the total mass of everything that travels (§6.3), cargo included. The server adds up each part's mass while it walks the assembly, so the cost is cheap to compute, and the HyperDrive shows it before the jump. Jumping out of a gravity well costs more (§3.4).

The HyperDrive draws the whole cost at once from the ship's power cells. Stored energy therefore sets how far a ship can jump, and a massive ship needs more cells to jump at all. Cells have mass of their own, so capacity has to be weighed against the cost of carrying it.

7. World Persistence

Changes are saved per chunk. A surface is divided into chunks 64 studs on a side, 16 terrain cells, and each chunk keeps its own list of changes. Loading a region replays only the chunks that have a list, and an untouched chunk still costs nothing.

Structures and inhabitants are planned for later, and the manifest is where they go. An NPC structure sits on terrain the same way a player's base does — it does not change how terrain generates. WoS put RobotDepot and RobotFactory in the planet type enum instead, which is why they are permanent placeholders in every universe generated since.

A destroyed pirate outpost is saved only as a destroyed flag in its region's save, never as its wreck: the outpost is generated from the region's seed, so it simply stops being generated (§3.4). Parts players captured from it are the exception: once locked, they are saved like any player's parts (§20.3).

A modified region keeps its generation. The first time a region saves anything (a change, a base, a flag), its save records which version of terrain generation made it. From then on it is always generated with that version, the old code and settings being kept alongside the new, so an update to generation never moves the ground under a base. Regions with no save use the newest version. A compacted chunk doesn't depend on generation at all, since its snapshot holds its voxels.

While a region is unloaded, clocks run and machines stop. A clock is anything that changes with time alone, needing no input, so its state after any gap can be worked out directly: corrosion (§13.6), tritium decay and an RTG core's decay (§16.2), an unpowered claim controller's store draining (§18.3), and a meltdown's radiation fading (§13.7). Each saves its state and the time with the region, and on load its state is worked out for the time that passed. Everything that runs on a network — production, consumption, charging, cooling, a reactor burning — stops where it was when the region unloads and carries on from there when it loads, as weapons do (§21).

A factory produces only while its region is loaded, so a held planet produces while someone is there.

8.2 Refining

Planets yield ores, not metals, and every metal is refined. How long the chain is follows how hard the metal really is to produce:

Furnace. A furnace takes fuel (coal or charcoal) and raw ore from its chute network and puts the refined metal back onto the chute network. Smelting works like any recipe: each metal takes its ore plus a set amount of fuel, pulled all at once, and harder metals take more fuel. With no ore to smelt, a furnace burns fuel for heat instead: each unit gives a fixed amount of heat, sent through a connected heatpipe (§14.4) whenever a heat-driven machine on it needs heat.

Evaporator. A heat-driven machine: it takes brine from a tank and heat through a heatpipe, drives off the water as steam to a tank or a machine that uses it, and leaves lithium salt for a bin or a machine that needs it.

Permanent magnets have poles. A permanent magnet part has a north face and a south face, on opposite sides along one axis. Facing poles that are alike repel and unlike ones attract, with a force that falls off quickly over a short range. Electromagnets have no poles and only attract. Poles are what let a permanent magnet push, so magnetic hovering, bearings and self-aligning docking are built from them.

Heat past its loss temperature demagnetizes a permanent magnet for good. It does not come back when the magnet cools (§16.1).

Alnico is the baseline magnet. Its strength and the temperature at which it loses its magnetism are fixed, and both are moderate: it is the magnet anyone can make, and too weak and too heat-sensitive to sit beside a plasma chamber (§16.2).

A magnet randmat rolls past it. The permanent magnet randmat (§9.3) rolls strength and loss temperature on separate curves, so most fall short of alnico on one of them and rare ones beat it on both. It can also roll one pole trait that alnico never has:

9. Randmats

A randmat is a material generated for the planet it is found on, with properties rolled from that planet's seed (§9.5). Materials from different planets are different materials, so randmats are something to discover, compare and collect. They give a reason to keep exploring: something other players envy, or a real advantage in base defense or offensive firepower.

9.1 Randmats in WoS

This subsection describes WoS only.

Properties observed in WoS: explodes on contact with a named material (coal, wood, stone); ignites on contact with a named material; emits light on contact with a named material; raises or lowers temperature; radioactive, causing radiation poisoning in proximity. Also unique reflectance, texture, durability and malleability — so a randmat can exceed titanium in strength, or permit larger part sizes than standard materials.

Several never worked. Burn color on ignition did not function. Temperature properties did little, because WoS's heat system was disabled.

9.2 Finding randmats

discussed, not settled

Randmats are common; good ones are rare. Many planets carry a randmat, but most rolls are mediocre and exceptional ones are rare. Exploring keeps paying off, and a great find is worth holding and fighting over (§18.3). Some planets roll none, which is what the starter filter selects on (§4.2).

Randmats deplete like every resource (§8.1), so their deposits are rich. A randmat that runs out almost immediately gets used sparingly and then lost, pushing players away from the interesting thing. The answer is not an endless supply but a deep one: a deposit's yield is set by its grade — how concentrated it is — not by how large it looks, and randmat deposits are high-grade, so a host planet provides a substantial amount before it is worked out. That makes an on-site factory worth building and defending: "I found a material" becomes "I hold the planet producing it, for now", which is a better reason for other players to care.

Properties are found by scanning. WoS prints a randmat's properties in its description. WastedSpace hides them until a sample is analyzed by a scanner part the player builds. With randmat properties expanding heavily, finding out what a material does becomes part of the game rather than a tooltip. The scanner is intended to be the same equipment that reads plant genes for gene splicing (§11), so one line of analysis parts serves both.

A scan is kept by the player and can be printed. What a player learns from a scan is saved with that player and shown whenever they look at that randmat. The scanner can also print it onto a data chip, an item that can be stored or traded, so a scout can sell what they found. The chip names the randmat by its code (§9.5), so it can't be passed off as a different material.

Randmats are also ingredients. Besides building with them, randmats go into recipes, including crystal growth for lenses (§10), so a rare randmat can give a product nobody else can make.

9.3 Properties

discussed, not settled

Traits hook into systems players already use (heat, power, logistics, combat, air), so a randmat is something to exploit rather than an isolated effect.

Base numbers. Every material has them, and a randmat rolls its own: durability, malleability, volume cap, density, heat resistance, heat spreading, heat tolerance, corrosion resistance, flammability and burn color, plus unique texture and reflectance. WoS already allowed randmats beyond titanium on these.

No ceiling. Each property rolls on a curve with no maximum. Most rolls are ordinary, and the better a value, the rarer it is, but any value can be beaten. The universe has no edge, so there is always a better material somewhere for a player who searches long enough. How fast the odds fall is set per property: the chance of a roll at least x times the typical value is about 1 in xα, so with α = 2 one randmat in a hundred is ten times typical and one in ten thousand is a hundred times. Properties where lower is better, such as density for a light material, roll the same way toward zero, approaching it without reaching it. Rolls go the other way too: a randmat's values can fall well below typical.

Effect curves. Where a raw value would break the game, its effect keeps growing but more slowly the higher it goes, so a better roll is always better and never unlimited: a durability far beyond anything yet found makes a very strong hull, not an invulnerable one. Two shapes cover it. A property that is a share of something, such as a resistance, approaches 100% without reaching it: its effect is x / (x + k), where x is the roll and k sets how fast it climbs. A property that is an amount, such as a volume cap or a rate of heat deletion, grows as a power below one, about x0.6, so a roll 100 times typical gives about 16 times the effect.

Triggers. WoS triggers were almost all contact with a named material (oil, water, stone). Any property could also switch on above or below a temperature, when powered or signaled, in vacuum, underwater, on impact, at speed, in sunlight, or in darkness. The combination of trigger and effect is what produces the unexpected — for example, a material that superconducts, but only in vacuum below −50 °C.

Triggers take engineering. A material needing an oil faucet to detonate can be found by anyone but weaponized only by players who build the rig to trigger it. The rig is visible on a ship, so the skill is legible to other players, and the payoff justifies the rig.

Sensors each give a plain trigger signal that works with the standard logic parts (switches, polysilicon), no microcontroller needed. That is what makes them useful for automation, drones and bots, which the game currently lacks options for.

Traits are few. A randmat carries none to about three, fewer being likelier, and lean tilts the count.

Traits suit the material. Numbers roll first, and they tilt which traits roll. A material whose numbers make it a hull or armor material, with exceptional durability or a clear armor use, favors traits that matter on a hull (damage resistance, ablative, self-repairing, reactive armor, laser reflection, damage as heat) and seldom rolls ones that would be wasted there, such as a sensor, a catalyst or oxygen release. A weak material leans the other way, toward traits used in small functional parts. Lean tilts it the same way: number-leaning randmats favor traits that build on their numbers. Unsuitable traits become rarer, not impossible, so the odd combination still turns up.

Sub-traits need their parent. Some traits only make sense on top of another: pole traits only on a magnetic randmat (§8.3), terrain-only and attuned damage only on an explosive one, a loss temperature only on a magnet or superconductor. A sub-trait rolls only if its parent did, and doesn't count toward the number of traits.

Triggers roll the same way traits do. Whether a trait has a trigger, and which one, is rolled per trait, weighted by what suits it. Traits that are about what a material is, such as damage resistance or the numbers of a hull material, rarely or never take one. Traits that do something are where triggers belong: a magnet, a light, a material that heats or cools might be always on, on only while powered, or on only when triggered by a signal, a temperature or a named material, depending on which makes sense for that trait. How traits and triggers are weighted, and how the weights are tuned, is in §9.4.

9.4 Weighting traits and triggers

discussed, not settled

A material's numbers decide which traits suit it. Once its numbers have rolled, a randmat is placed on a few axes that say what it is good for (structural, thermal, mass, conductive) and on its lean, and each trait is weighted by how well it suits that profile. Self-repairing is likely on a strong hull material and a proximity sensor is rare on one. Traits that push the same number opposite ways, such as insulator and vacuum radiator, never roll together.

A trait's kind decides which triggers it can take:

KindTriggers
Property — what the material is, such as damage resistanceRarely any
Continuous — something it keeps doing, such as a magnet, a light, heating or coolingStates: powered, signaled, above or below a temperature, in vacuum, underwater, in sunlight or darkness
One-shot — something that happens once, such as exploding, igniting or an EMPEvents: impact, contact with a named material, a signal pulse

A trigger the material contradicts never rolls. "Above 500 °C" never appears on a material that would take heat damage (§14) well below 500 °C.

A harder trigger makes a stronger trait. Each trigger has a cost: how hard a rig it takes to use, and how rarely its condition comes up on its own, and a triggered trait is stronger in proportion. Always on is 1×; contact with oil, which needs a faucet rig, earns a large multiplier; in sunlight, which needs nothing, earns little.

The odds are tuned to written targets, such as how often junk and materials good at everything appear, and that every allowed combination exists within reach. The game's rolls stay a deterministic hash of the address (§9.5).

9.5 Identity and transport

discussed, not settled

A randmat's properties come from its origin region's hash. Moving it to another region, another server, or a bin on the far side of the universe must not change or lose them.

The resolution is that properties are never transported. The address is. Derivation is deterministic and server-side, so any server can recompute a material's properties from its origin coordinates. Properties are not data that has to travel; they are a function of a location every server can evaluate.

AspectHow it works
StorageA stack in a bin or tank is {originRegion, materialIndex, quantity} — a few bytes. No property values are written to the DataStore, keeping randmats consistent with the zero-storage principle (§1.2): nothing is stored that can be re-derived.
StackingTwo stacks merge only if their origin and index match. Materials from different regions are different materials even when their properties are similar, so a bin holds them separately. The identity key is the address, not the property set.
Cross-server transportThe saved ship data carries the same address, never teleport data, which a player can replay. The destination server derives the properties itself on arrival. Nothing is synchronized between servers, because both compute the same answer from the same universe seed.
CachingA server derives a material's properties on first use and keeps them for its lifetime. Derivation is a few hashes, and the cache means a factory running thousands of operations per second pays that cost once.
Factory partsParts query properties, never identity. A heat sink asks for thermal conductivity and receives a number; whether it came from a crafted metal table or a hash roll is invisible to it.

Names. Every randmat has a code derived from its address, such as RM-7F3A-K9Q2, that no one can change, so a trade can't pass one material off as another. The first player to scan a randmat at its home planet can also name it. The name is saved with the material's address and shown with the code everywhere, for example Voidsteel [RM-7F3A-K9Q2].

10. Crystals & Lenses

discussed, not settled

A crafting system of its own, on the same footing as standard materials and parts, built entirely around lenses for energy weapons. It is the crystal counterpart to gene splicing (§11): players grow and design crystals rather than finding a finished product.

Custom energy weapons. Lenses are slotted into a base energy weapon to make it a specific weapon type — beam laser, pulse laser, and everything between. Lenses set damage, shot size, spread and its shape (cone, fan), rapid pulses, bursts, shotgun-style bursts, range, reload rate, area of effect, penetration and thermal behavior. They also set the beam's color: there is no painting a laser — to match a ship, a player grows the lens that produces that color.

Laser crystals heat up and need cooling, which ties the system into weapon heat (§14.7).

Two stages. A source crystal sets color, beam or pulse, pulse rate, damage and heat; shaping lenses set spread, focus, range and beam splitting. Custom weapon types come from combining them.

Production chain, in staged machines that feed each other:

  1. Raw crystal — mined from natural deposits, whose quality and flaws are whatever is found; or grown synthetically from feedstock (mined crystal, minerals, additives) under controlled heat, which is where design control lies.
  2. Cutter — cuts raw crystal to the required spec.
  3. Lens assembler — assembles cut crystal into a finished lens.

Crystal planets are the heart of it: crystal deposits of many types everywhere, alongside the ordinary metals needed to build the machines, so a crystal outpost can supply itself. Quartz and ruby are also among starter resources, so the basics are reachable from the start. Crystal deposits deplete like every resource (§8.1).

Randmats are dual-purpose — a building material, and an ingredient in recipes, including crystal growth, the way chromium turns sapphire into ruby. A rare randmat can give a crystal nobody else can grow.

Slotting is permanent. A lens is an item that can be traded or sold until it is slotted; once slotted, that is the weapon.

11. Plants & Gene Splicing

discussed, not settled

Plants are in at launch. A seed growing and gene splicing system.

Plants are the source of cloth. Fibers from grown plants are spun and woven into cloth, and cloth is a production line of its own: insulation, apparel (§16.4) and the other soft goods built from it. Rubber may also come from plants, as latex; that is still open.

Fits the game because it is the same shape of idea as randmats — find something with rolled properties, work out how to exploit it — but renewable where randmats are extractive and finite. If biospheres vary by biome, what grows where becomes another reason to hold territory.

Intended to feed the same systems as materials rather than running as a separate track: machinery, and thermal management in particular. Specifically raised — lining structures to create a livable ambient temperature on otherwise uninhabitable hot or cold worlds; encasing generators to manage heat creep; wrapping weapons that heat up to extend firing time. Same property, three scales of problem.

Insulation comes in three grades. Cloth insulation from plants is the everyday kind, renewable and grown wherever its crop grows. Crafted aerogel is the dense, high-end kind. An insulator randmat (§9.3) is the exceptional kind, found only on its planet. Hostile-world access is therefore renewable for anyone who farms, and the best of it is still worth holding a planet for.

12. Building

Building and terrain share a grid. The 1-stud build grid lines up with the 4-stud terrain cells. Filling terrain is refused in cells under placed parts; digging is always allowed, except on a planet someone else has claimed (§18.3).

Each terrain material digs at its own rate. The dig tool reads the material it hits and digs it at that material's rate, with that material's sound and debris: soil gives way fast and quietly, rock slowly with a crack, concrete slowest with a grinding scrape.

Bases stand on foundations. A foundation part attaches to a base's assembly, and clicking it pours a Cement footing: a frustum, widening downward, under the bottom faces of the assembly's parts and into the ground. A base on a foundation stays level when the ground around it is dug away. Digging removes terrain, never parts, so only weapons can break a footing. Cement is barely damaged by explosions, and the lasers that do break it are stopped by EnergyShields.

Pouring uses Cement by volume. A foundation draws Cement from its logistics network (§15), like a machine drawing an ingredient. Clicking it shows how much the footing needs, and it pours only once the network holds that much.

A base without a foundation rests on the ground. When the ground beneath it is dug out, it falls and takes normal impact damage.

A footing sets as it pours. It has full strength at once and needs no power.

Parts placed on terrain snap to the region's grid. A part placed on terrain, attached to nothing, snaps in position and size to the shared grid rather than to the uneven ground it lands on, so separately placed parts line up with each other. A part can extend into terrain: the rock it overlaps stays, hidden behind it, and placing never digs.

That is how a dug tunnel becomes a finished one. A Cement slab goes down level on the floor and is widened past the rough walls into the rock. Wall slabs snap to the same grid and are sized down to meet it, then the ceiling goes on the same way, leaving a flat, smooth lining that follows the tunnel as it was dug.

13. Volumes & Environment

Every part and every player is either inside a sealed volume or out in the ambient environment. Air, heat, corrosion and radiation are all worked out against that one distinction, so a hull does several jobs at once: it holds air, holds or keeps out heat, keeps out a corrosive atmosphere and shields radiation.

13.1 Volumes

A volume is a sealed interior, or the ambient environment outside it.

Sealing is what makes a volume exist. An open-top platform has no interior, so it sits at ambient — the flying board with an air supply bolted to it stops working, with nothing needed to forbid it. Airlocks, hatches and pressure doors become structural rather than decorative, and a hull breach drains air and equalizes temperature in one event.

The same breach lets a corrosive atmosphere in (§13.6), and the interior loses its hull's shielding against ambient radiation (§13.7).

The enclosure test is a flood fill over a grid of 1-stud cells, the size of the build grid (§12). A cell is solid when its center lies inside a part or inside rock, and air otherwise. Two neighboring air cells are joined unless the line between their centers passes through a part, tested against the part's own shape, so a wall seals however thin it is and angled armor seals where it actually sits. A volume is a set of joined air cells that does not reach ambient. On anything built on the 1-stud grid the test is exact: every hole a stud wide or more leaks. A gap narrower than a stud leaks only where a line between cell centers passes through it, so a build should not rely on one either way. Only placed parts that collide seal; characters, loose items and parts that do not collide never do.

Each ship has its own grid. A ship's grid is in its own frame and holds every part joined to it (§6.3), so its volumes move with it and are never worked out again because it moved. A part on a hinge, piston or rail is entered in the grid where it comes to rest and seals nothing while it moves, so a door built on a hinge seals when it swings shut. Anything that is not anchored has a grid of its own the same way, until it comes to rest against terrain (below).

Terrain, and everything anchored or resting on it, share the region's grid. It is aligned with the terrain cells, and a base on a foundation counts as anchored (§12). Only on this grid does rock close a volume (§13.8). A ship or base that comes to rest against terrain moves onto the region's grid, so parts closing a cave's openings seal it whether or not they are anchored. When it moves off again, it goes back to a grid of its own. Either move is one pass over that structure, the same as a ship built on arrival. A ship held anchored while it is rebuilt on arrival (§6.2) keeps its own grid. Rock is solid where players collide with it, which the dig test measured at half a terrain cell outside the voxel data, not where the voxels end. Water is not a wall: a volume open to water is open to ambient (§13.6).

Reaching ambient. On a ship's grid, ambient is everything outside the ship's bounding box. On the region's grid, a flood has reached ambient when it reaches a cell outside the box around every part on that grid, or a cell open to the sky, with no rock or part above it. A ship's ambient is whatever volume it sits in, so a ship parked in a sealed hangar opens into the hangar's air, not the planet's.

Doors are links, not moving walls. Door and hatch parts always count as closed in the grid, and each one links the two volumes on either side of it. Two volumes joined by an open door share air and temperature as one; a volume joined to ambient through any chain of open doors is breached. Opening and closing a door finds no volume again, so an airlock can cycle as often as it is used at no cost.

The grid is kept in chunks of 16 × 16 × 16 cells, allocated only where there are parts, rock beside air, or flooded cells; a chunk wholly inside one volume stores one label instead of 4,096. A flood crosses a chunk holding nothing but air in a single step, and visits single cells only in chunks with walls, rock or parts in them. Its cost therefore follows the surface around a space, not the air inside it. A hangar 512 studs on a side holds 134 million cells, but a check costs about what its 1.6 million wall cells do, estimated at under half a second on a Roblox server. There is no limit on how large a volume can be. Floods run under the per-frame work budget (§15.2), so a large check, such as closing off a whole cave network, spreads across frames instead of stalling one, and a volume keeps its old state until its check finishes. Crossing chunks in one step has not been measured yet.

Three other tests were rejected. Casting rays outward from a point misses small holes and gives a volume no lasting identity to hold air in. Flooding the whole grid on every change would make every door cycle check the whole ship again. A coarser grid of 4-stud cells, matching terrain, is larger than a small ship's rooms and corridors.

13.2 Boundaries

A volume's boundary is its hull: the layers of parts between inside and outside. Each layer's material contributes three things:

PropertyHow the hull uses it
Heat resistanceHow much it slows heat crossing the boundary. Adds up across the layers (§14.5).
Radiation shieldingThe material's density times its thickness. Adds up across the layers (§13.7).
Corrosion resistanceOnly the outer layer, which the atmosphere touches, is exposed (§13.6).

All three are worked out per boundary when it changes and then cached, so a standing hull costs nothing to evaluate.

Solid terrain is a boundary too: rock closes a volume the way a wall does (§13.8).

13.3 Ambient

Ambient is what a region supplies outside any volume, rolled from its biome's weights (§4.2): a temperature, an atmosphere (none, thin, normal or thick), what its air is (breathable, unbreathable, toxic, corrosive or spores), a corrosion rate and a radiation level. Space is ambient too, with almost no capacity to carry heat away.

Ambient is not a constant. Its baseline comes from the biome, and it moves through the day/night cycle (§3.2) around that baseline — tidally locked planets have no cycle, so their ambient holds steady at their fixed time of day.

Atmosphere sets how far the day swings. Air holds heat, so the thinner it is, the hotter the day and colder the night: usually about 3 °C either side of the baseline under thick air, 8 under normal air, 25 under thin air and 100 with none. Each planet rolls its own swing around those.

Heat dumped outside raises ambient above its baseline, and ambient drifts back down over time. That gives outside dumping a limit: a large enough operation warms the region it sits in, the gradient shrinks as ambient rises, and radiators shed more slowly the more you run. An industrialized planet is hotter than an untouched one, and cooling gets harder as you scale.

13.4 Weather

Clear weather is the norm. Each biome sets how much of the time its planets have weather, and each planet rolls its own share from that (§4.2). A Terra planet is clear most of the time, a Desert is mostly clear between sandstorms, and a Swamp is often foggy. No biome has weather all the time.

Weather comes in spells. A spell lasts from minutes to hours of real time, and builds up and fades over a couple of minutes, so the sky changes the way real weather does rather than flickering.

Most weather is mild. Light rain is common, downpours less so, and violent storms rare. Fog usually thins the view to a few hundred studs; only rare dense fog brings it under 50, and no weather blinds a player to a few feet.

Each biome has its own weather:

Weather comes from the planet's seed and the clock, the same way time of day does (§3.2). Every server agrees on the weather at any moment, nothing is stored, and how long a spell has left can be known.

13.5 Air

Air is a property of a volume, the same as temperature. A supply pressurizes the sealed space it occupies rather than projecting a radius through walls, and both systems use the same enclosure test.

Supply size means capacity, not reach — a larger one pressurizes a larger volume, or the same volume faster. On its own, a supply pressurizes only the volume it sits in; other sections get their air through vents.

Vents carry air between sections. A vent is a wall panel on a pipe, and it pressurizes the sealed volume it opens into. An Air Supply sends air down its pipes to every vent on that network, so one supply, or several, can pressurize a whole ship through its bulkheads without equipment in every room. A vent draws air only while its volume is below full, and air never flows back into the pipe; when supply runs short, vents share it fairly (§15.2). A breached room loses its own air, and its vent keeps feeding it until the room is sealed or a valve on that branch is closed, while the rest of the ship keeps its air.

Planets with breathable atmosphere need no supply at all.

An Air Supply needs oxygen piped into it. It uses oxygen to fill a sealed volume and to replace air lost when the volume is opened or breached; a sealed, intact volume stays breathable without more. New ships start with a filled oxygen tank; after that, oxygen comes from electrolysis (§16.3).

13.6 Corrosion

Corrosion is carried by what fills a volume, like air. Toxic atmospheres and seawater corrode the parts they touch, so parts outside a sealed volume or underwater corrode, and parts inside a sealed interior do not. A breach lets the corrosive atmosphere in with the same event that drains the air (§13.1).

Each biome has a corrosion rate, zero on most worlds, and each material a corrosion resistance. Titanium, gold, lead and glass resist it; iron corrodes quickly.

Staged, not ticked. An exposed part has a corrosion age. Past one threshold it is corroded, visibly rusted and weaker; past a second it is severely corroded, heavily rusted, with its durability cut so far that weapons and impacts break it easily. Corrosion alone never destroys a part. The server schedules only each part's next stage change, worked out when its exposure starts or changes, and never schedules resistant parts, so corrosion costs nothing per frame. The rust texture blends with age, so a part visibly worsens before its stage changes.

Corrosion continues while a region is unloaded. Stage times are saved, and any that came due are applied when the region loads.

Repair winds the clock back. The repair tool and repair laser reduce a part's corrosion age in proportion to the repair applied, so a partial repair leaves it less corroded and a full repair restores it completely. Repair fixes combat damage first, then corrosion.

13.7 Radiation

Radiation is not carried by air. It passes through walls, reduced by the material in the way, and shielding depends on density and thickness: lead, a thick stone wall or a dense randmat all block it. A boundary's shielding is the sum of its layers, worked out and cached with its insulation (§13.2).

SourceBehavior
Radioactive materialsUranium, plutonium and radioactive randmats irradiate their surroundings, falling off with distance and reduced by any boundaries in between.
ReactorsA source while running. A meltdown irradiates the area around it, fading over time.
Irradiated worldsAmbient radiation throughout the region.

Against ambient radiation, only a sealed volume shields its interior, through its hull's layers. An open structure gives no protection.

Dose. Exposure adds to a player's accumulated dose, which fades slowly. While dose is above a threshold, max health is reduced, more at higher doses. The Geiger counter reads the local radiation level.

Plated apparel. Apparel can carry protective plating, which reduces radiation by its material's density and thickness like any other shielding. A full suit of thick lead plating blocks it completely.

13.8 Underground

Rock buffers the surface. Underground, ambient temperature depends on depth below the terrain surface: the day/night swing fades a short way into the rock, and below that the temperature holds steady at the average of the day/night cycle. Deeper still it rises slowly on a geothermal gradient the biome weights (§4.2), steep on Volcanic worlds and gentle on Glacial ones. A tidally locked world has no night to average in, so its underground is as hot or as cold as its surface.

Rock is a wall. Solid terrain counts as a boundary for the enclosure test (§13.1), so a cave becomes a sealed volume once its openings are closed with parts. The rock does the work of a hull: it is thick enough to block radiation (§13.7), and it never corrodes, so only the parts closing the openings are exposed (§13.6).

Rock takes heat. It holds the underground temperature, so walls against it shed heat into it like any colder neighbor (§14.3), and a sealed cave sheds its machines' heat into the surrounding rock without radiators. The rock around a busy cave warms the way ambient does (§13.3), so a large operation still needs them.

Together these make mines the cheapest place to live on a hostile world: what is expensive on the surface is free underground. The surface keeps sunlight, open space for large ships and easy access.

14. Heat

Heat is standard gameplay. It gates hostile biomes, limits how fast weapons can fire, forces factories to be engineered rather than assembled, and gives randmat thermal properties something to act on.

14.1 Temperature

Temperatures are kept in kelvin and shown in the player's choice of unit. The game works in kelvin, so absolute zero is 0 and no temperature is negative. Players choose °C, °F or K, and every gauge, setting and readout shows temperatures in that unit.

Every part has its own temperature. Parts trade heat with the parts they touch and with the volume they sit in — a sealed interior, or the ambient environment outside it (§13.1).

A volume's temperature is its accumulated energy against its heat capacity. A small sealed room heats quickly, a large one slowly, so factory layout matters without a rule saying so.

14.2 Cooling

Heat is created — a furnace burning fuel, a generator running, a weapon firing. Ordinary parts never delete it: they spread it out or move it elsewhere, in tiers.

PartsWhat they do
CoolersShort range, like a fan. A cooler takes a hot part's heat and spreads it across the parts and air around it, cooling the hot part without moving heat out of the area. While the source keeps running, the area as a whole still heats up. In vacuum there is no air to take it, so a cooler spreads heat only into the parts around it.
Heat pumps and heatpipesPump heat out of an area to somewhere else: a radiator, or a heat store (§14.4).
RadiatorsDiscard heat into ambient (§13.3).
Heat storesPlayer-built: copper spreads heat into materials with a high heat capacity, wrapped in strong insulation or a randmat, holding it until it can be dumped.

WoS coolers simply lower temperature while powered, which makes any heat transport pointless. Here spreading heat only buys time, so anything that keeps running needs a way to pump its heat out and dump or store it. Building that chain is the engineering.

Randmats are the exception. A randmat can delete heat outright while powered, a deliberate departure from realism that a rare find earns. How much it deletes rolls like any other property (§9.3).

14.3 Where heat goes

A part is only ever inside a volume or out in ambient. Placement decides its destination, not its type. The same emitter heats a room when it is inside one, warms the planet when it is outside, and radiates into vacuum in space.

Efficiency follows the environment and the gradient. Thick atmosphere carries heat away quickly, thin atmosphere barely does, and vacuum is slowest of all — which is why a ship's ability to shed heat limits how much power it can run.

Since ambient rises as heat is dumped into it (§13.3), a part outside does not warm the planet in any permanent sense; it loses heat to a sink that pushes back as it fills.

Underground, walls against rock shed heat into the rock (§13.8).

14.4 Moving heat

Carried by air. Moving gas moves its temperature with it. Pump warm air out of a factory into a chilled chamber and pump it back — the chiller only has to cool one small volume rather than the whole base.

Carried by heatpipe. Energy moves with no mass — heatpipes carry heat only, and are a separate network from the pipes that transport water, gasoline, steam and other fluids. This is what lets a sealed underground base run combustion generators: heat leaves through a heatpipe to a surface radiator without any opening, so no pressure or breathable air is lost. If heat could only ride on gas, dumping it would mean venting the room.

Heatpipes are configurable as hot or cold transport, so a base runs a hot loop out to radiators and a cold loop back from chillers, and parts connect to whichever they need.

14.5 Materials and insulation

Each material carries two independent numbers.

Resistance is how much it slows heat crossing a boundary. Lateral conductivity is how readily it equalizes with adjacent parts of the same material.

These are separate properties and the interesting builds come from combining them. Copper spreads a hot spot across an entire surface the way copper cookware does. Titanium resists transfer but does not distribute. So a copper inner shell spreads a point source across every wall, a titanium layer outside slows the transfer with no single point under load, and aerogel or cement behind that slows what remains.

A boundary's total resistance is the sum of its layers, which is how real thermal engineering works and is cheap to compute because it is per boundary rather than per part. The same layers also shield radiation and resist corrosion (§13.2).

Lateral equalization is per-part, but narrowly so: one pass over a connected run of the same material, cached until something changes.

14.6 Phase transitions

Matter changes state with temperature:

  • Water becomes ice in a cold enough environment.
  • Water becomes steam in a hot enough one.
  • Ice becomes water when warmed, and steam becomes water when cooled.

Steam is a gas cube that rises, and tanks and bins capture loose items they come into contact with. Players build custom boilers on this: an automatically fueled fire, water sprayed onto it by faucets or ice dropped onto it by dispensers, and a tank above to catch the steam.

A phase change alters an entity's state. It does not destroy one entity and create another. Velocity therefore carries through the transition: ice pushed fast down a copper tube becomes steam still traveling at that speed, and leaves the tube. Buoyancy is a force applied to gas-state matter on top of inherited motion, not a fixed rise speed replacing it.

WoS destroys and recreates, which is why steam stops dead on transition, overlaps with no collision, and trickles into a capture tank one cube at a time. Condensation works the same way in reverse.

14.7 Combat and industry

Weapons heat when fired and fail until they cool (§16.1), and heat each other by proximity. Packing cheap guns into a compact block becomes self-defeating, because density is what stops them firing. A spread array with cooling beats a brick, and that falls out of the physics rather than a balance rule. A shot heats its gun in proportion to its damage (§21.1).

Machinery produces constant heat. In the open it goes straight into ambient (§13.3). Inside a sealed volume it heats the room, and the room conducts heat out through its walls to whatever is outside, faster the hotter it gets inside, so its temperature levels off where what the walls shed matches what the machines produce. A small room with a few machines levels off somewhere survivable; a room packed with machines, or one behind heavy insulation, levels off above lethal, so cooling becomes part of building a factory as it grows.

15. Logistics

Items, fluids, power and heat are the same system with different transport parts. Each is a resource moving through a graph of connected parts, with its own switches, gates, containers and sinks:

Liquids and gasesPipes, filters, valves, diodes, sortersTransportersTanksFaucets, vents
PowerWires, switches, diodes, sortersAntennasPower cellsLoads

Dispensers and faucets release physical items (solids, liquids, or gas you can see and touch until it dissipates) slowly, and only while switched on, by hand, a trigger or a microcontroller. Vents work passively and much faster, and release no items: the gas becomes part of a volume's air (§13.5).

They differ in what flows and which parts connect, not in how the network behaves. One implementation covers all four: a wire network and a chute network are the same solver with a different resource type, and switch, gate and valve parts stop needing per-resource variants.

Heat is included deliberately. It is not a separate system from the rest of §14 — it is a resource network whose resource is heat, and a radiator is its sink the way a dispenser is for solids.

15.1 Why WoS lags

This subsection describes WoS only.

Every tick, every part independently runs a breadth-first search across the whole network for each resource its recipe needs, one search per ingredient, crafts its recipe, then searches the entire network again for somewhere to put the output — continuing until it finds a bin for solids or a tank for liquids. Every bin it takes from or puts into then has its item count updated as well.

Machines also cannot hand items to each other directly. Every output has to go into a bin before the next machine can take it — a pulverizer touching a kiln does nothing until a sand bin is placed between them or shared on the same network.

The number of parts searching and the size of the network each one searches both grow with the factory, so doubling a factory roughly quadruples the work. This is why medium and large builds lag a region to death: it is the wrong complexity class, not a tuning problem.

A dynamic tick rate was added later as a mitigation, throttling from 20/sec to 1/sec on load. It oscillates badly between the two extremes and does not address the cause — each part still walks the entire network, just less often, so throughput collapses while per-search cost stays the same.

15.2 Indexed, event-driven networks

A resource network is a graph problem, not a movement problem. Parts are nodes, connections are edges, and topology only changes when a player builds or breaks something.

Measured against WoS. Both approaches were simulated on the same randomly built factories, from 100 to 10,000 parts on one connected chute network: chutes, bins, extractors and assemblers, two ores and ten part recipes of two to four ingredients in amounts of one to five. Every craft runs every step in both versions: find bins holding each ingredient until the full amount is covered, deduct the amounts, find a bin already holding the output with room or else an empty bin that accepts it, and add the output to it. Extractors do the last two steps for their ore.

The WoS version finds bins by walking the network from the machine, one walk per search. The WastedSpace version reads the network's index, updated whenever a bin changes. Both made exactly the same number of items at every size, so they did the same work.

Solids, liquids and gasesThe producer sleeps when nothing on its network can take its output — no machine needs it and no bin or tank has room — and wakes when something can.

15.4 On the dynamic tick rate

Stepping more gradually is better than the 20-to-1 cliff, but oscillation is a feedback problem and finer steps alone will not stop it: the measurement that triggers throttling is the thing throttling changes. If a throttle is kept it needs hysteresis (different thresholds for slowing down and speeding up), asymmetric response (drop fast, recover slowly), and decisions made on a rolling average rather than the instantaneous frame.

Better outcome is not needing one. With event scheduling there is no global tick to throttle — an idle factory already costs nothing. A throttle is worth keeping only as a backstop under genuine overload.

16.1 Operating states

Every working part is in one of five states.

StateMeaningEnds when
RunningDoing its job.—
WaitingNothing it can do: no input to work on, or finished output with nowhere to go (§15.3).Input arrives, or something can take its output.

Failing is a pause, not damage. A failed part stops working and comes back by itself once the condition clears; it takes no damage from failing. Destroyed is the only permanent end. A part's networks see a failed part the same as a waiting one: it stops drawing and producing, and nothing else needs to know why.

What fails a part. Each part type lists the conditions it fails under:

A part fails before it is damaged. A part has an operating limit and a heat tolerance. Above its operating limit it fails and pauses; above its heat tolerance, which is higher, it takes damage until it is destroyed or cools (§14). Running hot therefore stops a part before it starts to hurt it. The operating limit comes mostly from the part type (electronics fail cooler than a furnace lining), and never exceeds its material's heat tolerance. Weapons stopping until they cool (§14) is this rule.

A part recovers a margin below where it failed. It fails at its limit but runs again only once the condition is back past the limit by a set margin, so it doesn't flicker at the boundary. The restart time is set per part type, and restarting costs nothing.

Failure and randmat triggers share their checks (§9.4): "above 300 °C" switching a trait on and "above 300 °C" failing a machine are the same check.

Entering failure can have a consequence. Most failures only pause the part. A part type can attach an effect to the moment it fails: a fusion reactor's confinement failing dumps the plasma's energy as a burst of heat (§16.2).

Failure is visible and can be wired. A failed part looks different. It reports its state and the reason to a microcontroller (§17), and can send a trigger signal when it fails and when it recovers, so alarms and automatic shutdowns can be built from standard logic parts.

A permanent change is not a failure. A permanent magnet heated past its loss temperature loses its magnetism for good, even after it cools (§8.3). It is demagnetized, a lasting change to the part, not a state it recovers from.

16.2 Power cells and reactors

Power cells come in three tiers, each needing a material from a harder world:

TierBuilt withFound on
1ZincStarter and temperate planets

Fission. The Reactor is a fission reactor, as in WoS: uranium, or thorium (§8.1), heats it, and it turns water into steam for SteamTurbines. Lithium target rods placed in a fission reactor slowly breed tritium. Spent rods go into an extractor, which heats them and releases the tritium as a gas into a tank.

Fusion. A fusion reactor is built, not placed. Players build it around a plasma chamber: magnets confine the plasma, a lithium lining with beryllium breeds tritium, and shielding stops its neutrons (§13.7). It burns deuterium, from heavy water (§8.1), with tritium, and produces plasma, which drives a new tier of power generation parts. The build decides how it runs: the magnets set its output and stability, the lining sets its breeding ratio, and the shielding sets how much radiation escapes. Tritium bred in the lining flows out as a gas like any other, to be piped back into the reactor or stored. A lining that breeds less tritium than the reactor burns slowly runs it dry, since fission-bred tritium alone cannot keep up. Stored tritium slowly decays into helium in its tank, so it cannot be stockpiled to make up the difference; the decay continues while the owner is offline. Electromagnets need power and must be kept cold beside the plasma (§14.2); permanent magnets need neither but are weaker. If confinement fails, because a magnet loses power, fails from heat, is demagnetized or is destroyed, the stored energy of the plasma and magnets dumps into the reactor at once as a burst of heat that damages it and nearby parts, and a damaged reactor leaks tritium gas. The gas contaminates the air of the volume it leaks into (§13.1) and harms anyone breathing it without their own air, until the volume is vented; plating does not protect against it. Once that burst is over, nothing keeps heating.

RTG. A radioisotope generator: a plutonium core whose decay heat a thermocouple turns straight into power. It needs no fuel feed, no water and no cooling rig, so it suits probes, drones, beacons and remote outposts. Its output is low and falls slowly as its core decays, and the decay continues while its region is unloaded (§7). It gives off heat and radiation like any plutonium source (§13.7), so it needs shielding where players work. A spent core is replaced with a new one.

16.4 Personal equipment

Temperature only harms a character outside 0 to 54 °C. Below freezing a character takes cold damage, and above 54 °C heat damage; in between it can be miserable but is never hurt. Insulated suits and personal heaters extend the range a character can work in.

An air tank worn on apparel keeps the character breathing outside a breathable volume. It refills inside one, or anywhere the ambient air is breathable, drawing from that volume's air, and slowly empties everywhere else. Time outside is limited by the tank. It is a different mechanic from pressurizing a space, not a smaller version of one.

Power comes over antennas, which bridge electricity and trigger signals wirelessly. The same link carries lights, so hands stay free for building tools, and personal heaters. Heat is created, so a heater works against cold; a cooler only spreads heat around (§14.2), so there is no personal cooler.

This is what makes construction viable on hostile worlds. Without it, building outside needs a hangar larger than the ship, so cost scales with the thing being built. With it you build in the open, limited by your tank and power.

Enclosure requirements then fall out in three tiers:

PlanetRequirement
Breathable, temperateNothing
Unbreathable, temperateAir tank, build in the open between refills
Unbreathable, hostile temperatureHangar — a tank keeps you breathing, not alive

The hardest worlds are the ones where a large ship is genuinely expensive to build.

Radiation adds its own requirement on Irradiated worlds: plated apparel or shielded structures (§13.7), whatever the world's air and temperature.

Suits are built in layers. Apparel is worn per limb (head, torso, arms and legs) and parts attach to it, so a suit is a personal hull built from the same layers as any other (§13.2). Heat resistance adds up across its layers, so insulation, aerogel or a randmat slow heat getting in or out; lead shields radiation (§13.7); and the outer layer sets corrosion resistance (§13.6). A suit slows heat rather than stopping it, so long work on the hottest worlds still needs a hangar.

A Suit Rack holds all six pieces in place on a frame shaped like a character, so a suit is built where its layers can be seen together, and put on from the rack.

Every layer adds mass. A heavier suit slows its wearer and hits with its full weight: a lead suit moves slowly and is hard to stop. In WoS a thick lead suit runs at full speed and sends heavy parts flying like a juggernaut; here its weight works both ways, so players tailor a suit to the world instead of wearing the heaviest one everywhere.

17. Scripting

discussed, not settled

WastedSpace has its own scripting API. Microcontroller scripts are written against an API designed for WastedSpace and released under its own name, not against WoS's Pilot.lua. It changes Pilot.lua where WastedSpace's parts and systems need it, rather than staying compatible with it.

17.1 Documentation

The reference can't drift from the game. Every method, event and type is declared once, in definitions the game itself loads, and the reference pages, the editor's sidebar and autocomplete (below), and type definitions for external editors are all generated from them. Each method and event lists exactly which parts have it; a removed or renamed one is listed as such, with what replaces it.

17.2 Parts in scripts

Every part keeps one GUID for its whole life. A part gets its GUID when it is placed, and keeps it through region unloads and loads and through transfers between regions, until it is destroyed or picked up. The GUID is saved with the part in its region's manifest or its ship's record (§6.3). A script can store a GUID and find the same part with it later.

A part's events include its components'. A component installed on a part, such as a click or key button, adds its events to that part, and a script subscribes to them on the part the same way as to the part's own. A Button is an ordinary part that comes with those components installed; a script can't tell it apart from any other part with the same components.

Every part's color can be read. A script can read any part's color. It can set the color of parts whose color is a display, such as lights and bins.

A bin shows what it holds, and goes back to its own color when emptied. A script can also set or clear a bin's color itself.

Text typed into a screen reaches the script. A text box on a Screen, Touchscreen or AR display sends what the player types to the server, where the script that drew the box receives it with the player who typed it, both when the player submits and, if the script asks, as they type. No Keyboard part is needed. Only a player allowed to use the screen (§18.2) can type into it.

Parts report their operating state. A script can read any connected part's state and the reason it failed, and be told when either changes, rather than checking in a loop (§16.1).

17.3 The script editor

Scripts are written in an in-game editor with the API beside them. The editor is a window with the script on one side and the full API reference in a sidebar, so a player never needs to leave the game to look something up. The sidebar and the editor's autocomplete are generated from the same definitions as the reference (§17.1), so they always match the game.

It works like a code editor. Code is syntax-highlighted beside a line-number gutter. Autocomplete suggests methods, enums and values as you type, and accepting a call selects its first argument so you can type straight over it. Several scripts open side by side in tabs, and a console shows each script's output and errors.

17.4 Running scripts

Player code never runs directly. A script is compiled and run inside an interpreter, so it reaches only the API it is given and nothing else on the server.

A script can't freeze its server. The interpreter counts the instructions a script runs and makes it pause after every few hundred, so a busy script shares the server's time and an endless loop only ever runs in short slices. Each script also has limits on its total instructions, its memory and how many modules it loads; past a limit it stops with an error in the console.

The Event API that scripts events (§3.6) is a separate API for the game's own content.

open the API's name; the rest of the changes from Pilot.lua; its full method list.

18. Gameplay

Progression is only what you have. There are no levels, research trees or unlocks. A player is further along because of their materials, their builds, what they know how to engineer, and the planets they hold. A new player who gets the materials can build anything.

Trade is barter. There is no currency. Players exchange materials, parts and ships directly, at whatever they agree on. Exchange is physical — dropping items, loading bins, handing over ships — with no trade window. Trust is part of the game, and so is being scammed.

18.1 In-game editor

The resize tool has four step modes: 1, 0.5, 0.25 and 0.1. The 0.25 mode snaps to quarters (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.00), so models built on a quarter grid in Studio can be edited in the game.

18.2 Lock groups

Lock groups are the permission system. A player creates lock groups and owns them, as many as they like. A group has a name and a list of members, each a player or a faction (§19). A part is locked by at most one group. Only that group's members can act on a locked part, each only as far as they are granted. A locked part can still be broken by normal damage.

An unlocked part is open to everyone, including to locking it. Parts don't record who built them; the lock is what makes them someone's. Handing over a ship is unlocking it and letting the new owner lock it (§18).

A part or a whole assembly is locked at once. The lock tool locks a single part, or every part welded to it in one action.

Permissions are granted per member. Each member of a group holds its own set of these permissions, and a new member starts with the group's default set:

PermissionAllows
UseOperating the part: sitting in it, piloting from it, opening it, holding it
TriggerFiring it by hand, and wiring it so other parts trigger it
AccessTaking from and putting into its storage: bins, tanks, inventories
ConfigureChanging its settings and its microcontroller code
PaintChanging its color and material
ResizeUsing the resize tool on it (§18.1)
AttachBuilding onto it or welding to it
ModifyMoving, unwelding or removing it
DigOn a planet claimed by a controller this group locks: digging and filling terrain (§18.3)
LockAdding parts to the group, and taking them out
ManageAdding and removing members and granting permissions, except Manage itself

Different levels of trust come from different groups: a guest group can lock a base's doors while an engineer group locks its reactor room.

The owner keeps every permission. A player's own group can't remove its owner, and the owner can hand the group to another member.

Deleting a group unlocks every part in it, wherever those parts are. The game asks for confirmation first.

Losing a permission takes effect at once, in every region where the group's parts are loaded. A player who loses Use is put out of the seat, and a tool action under way stops.

18.3 Planet claims

Planets are claimed. A player claims a planet by building a claim controller on its surface. While the claim stands, only members of the lock group that locks the controller, and holding Dig, can dig or change the planet's terrain (§18.2). Anyone else can still land and fight, but can only reach an underground base through its entrances, by breaking its bulkheads and beating its defenses. The universe has no edge, so locking a whole planet always leaves others another one like it.

A rare find belongs to its finder. While the claim holds, nobody else can mine the deposit. Others can trade for what it produces, or raid the facilities and take what is stored there.

The controller runs on stored power. It charges slowly from its power network, so it can't be filled at once. Without power its store drains on its own, and when the store is empty the claim lapses. Attackers can cut its power, and they can connect to the controller to drain it faster than it drains on its own. The drain is faster than the charge, so it works even while the owner's power is still connected. The store's size sets how long a siege takes, and how long an owner has to respond.

A claim lasts as long as its power. There is no inactivity timer. A claim whose owner has quit holds until someone wrecks its power supply and waits out the store.

Starter planets can't be claimed. Otherwise a veteran could lock new players out of digging the basic resources they start with.

19. Factions

A faction is a named group of players. Any player can found one, at no cost. Its name is unique, and so is its tag of 2 to 5 characters, shown beside its members' names.

A player belongs to at most one faction. A player joins by accepting an invitation, or by sending a join request that the faction accepts, and can leave at any time. Leaving or being removed ends everything the faction granted them at once (§18.2).

A faction has a leader and ranks. The faction names its ranks and orders them, such as Officer, Member and Recruit. Each rank is granted any of these faction permissions:

Faction permissionAllows
InviteInviting players, and accepting join requests
RemoveRemoving members of a lower rank
Manage ranksCreating, renaming, ordering and granting permissions to ranks below its own
Manage lock groupsRunning the faction's lock groups, within the limits in §18.2

Only the leader can rename or disband the faction, or hand the lead to another member. If the leader leaves, the highest-ranked member who has been in the faction longest becomes leader.

An absent leader is replaced. If the leader hasn't played for 30 days, the lead passes to the highest-ranked member who has been in the faction longest and has played in that time. A leader who returns later rejoins at the highest rank below leader.

A disbanded faction's lock groups pass to its leader. They become the leader's own groups, so nothing they lock is left open.

A deleted rank's lock-group rules move to the rank above it. Reordering ranks doesn't change any rule: each rule follows its rank, not its place in the order.

19.1 Shared lock groups

A faction joins lock groups (§18.2) as a member, and can own them.

A faction member can be limited to a rank. A group can list a faction down to a rank, such as "Officers and above get Modify", alongside the whole faction getting Use. A player listed both by name and through their faction holds both sets.

A faction can own lock groups. Members whose rank has Manage lock groups run them, so a faction's bases, claims and ships don't depend on one player staying. They can grant only permissions they hold, and can't change the rules for their own rank or any above it. The faction leader always holds every permission on the faction's groups and can't be removed from them. Only the leader can delete a faction group or hand it over.

20. NPC Factions

Players share the universe with two factions of NPCs, pirates and aliens. Both hold territory, both have sites throughout it, and every one of their sites works by the same rules (§20.3). Pirates are the mid-game threat; aliens are the late game, stronger and more advanced, with better rewards.

20.1 Pirates

Pirates are ruthless, aggressive and greedy. They don't wait to be found: they come to players and raid their bases for what is stored there.

Pirates have territory. Pirate presence is a value of each sector, sampled from a smooth field the way system density is (§2.2). Pirates gather where the prey is: the edges of clusters, the routes between them and the space around wormhole ends (§3.5). Cluster cores and the depths of voids see fewer. Presence sets how many pirate sites a region rolls and how tough they are, so heading into pirate space is a choice.

Pirate sites turn up in every kind of region:

WherePirate sites
Deep spaceOutposts, derelicts and ambushes (§3.4), hidden stations in rock fields, and fuel depots on long routes
SystemsHideouts in asteroid fields, and patrols near busy planets
Planet surfacesBuried caches, surface camps, crashed ships, and crews working a deposit they have taken over (§5)
Coming to playersRaids on bases, run as events in the region (§3.6), and convoys to hunt

A site's size follows presence. Pirate sites run from a small camp to a fortress, and the higher a sector's presence, the larger and better defended its sites tend to be.

20.2 Aliens

Aliens are more advanced, and formidable when provoked. They have no need to raid, though they aren't above it. Their ships and sites outclass anything pirates field, and so does what they hold, so beating them is the best-paid fight in the game.

Aliens are cybernetic. No alien walks or sits at a helm: each alien ship is flown by its alien core, and the core is the alien itself. That is why alien ships fly with nobody aboard.

An alien core is taken only by destroying its ship. The ship is the alien, and it has no wish to become anyone's part.

Aliens build with their own technology. Their ships and sites carry weapons, shields and other parts unlike anything humans make, recognizably alien and more advanced.

Alien material is forged, never mined. It is made in a machine built around an alien core, the way titanium is refined rather than dug up (§8.2). It is strong and resilient, the late-game counterpart of titanium for hulls and armor, and the precursor every alien part is made from, in quantity. One machine forges it steadily but slowly; more output takes more machines, and every machine takes a core, so aliens stay worth fighting long after a player's first alien ship.

A woken core takes over a ship. Installed in place of a seat in a ship whose parts are wired together (§8.4), a core takes control of every part its network reaches and flies the ship as an alien. Its temper depends on what it wakes in. In a ship built from human parts it wakes angry and attacks everything around it, its builder first. In a ship built from alien parts it is content, and acts like any alien: neutral until someone fires on it, then formidable. Between the two, the larger the share of alien parts its network reaches, the calmer it is.

Aliens have territory of their own, set by their own presence value the same way. Their sites are fleets, strongholds and ruins in deep space (§3.4), and alien sites on planet surfaces, built in black faceted geometry (§5.6).

20.3 NPC sites

Every NPC site is a hand-built model. Each faction has its own style, and the seed varies each copy.

A site is locked until its claim controller falls. Each site has its own lock group (§18.2), held by a claim controller inside it, the same part that claims a planet (§18.3). While the controller stands and has power, players can damage the site but can't lock, modify or use any of it. Destroying the controller, or cutting its power and draining it as with any claim, deletes the group, which unlocks every part in it, including pieces broken loose. The site is then cleared.

What players lock is theirs. A cleared site's parts are open to anyone to lock. Parts a player locks, a whole assembly at once if they like, are saved with the region like parts they placed, and can be moved, modified or flown away. Whatever is still unlocked when the region unloads is gone, since a cleared site's parts never generate again.

A cleared site's ground stays. A cleared site is normally skipped when its region loads, but one that carves terrain still carves its rooms and tunnels and pours its Concrete shell (§5.6). Only what goes inside is skipped, its parts, defenders and loot, so a raided facility is left as a plain concrete shell.

Loot comes from the faction's tables. Each faction fills its sites' containers from its own loot tables, scaled with the site's size.

21. Combat

Bases are always vulnerable, including while their builders are offline. Bases persist with the region (§7), so automated defences — turrets, microcontroller-driven systems — are the only protection while nobody is home.

Weapons keep their charge and heat through reloads. A weapon's charge and temperature are saved with the region, like the contents of a bin (§7), so a region loads with its defenses as ready, and as hot, as they were when it unloaded. Nothing charges or cools while a region is unloaded.

21.1 Guns

A gun's face sets its damage, and its length sets its range. The width × height of a gun's cross-section sets the damage of each shot. Its length sets how far the shot reaches, growing with length up to a cap.

A short, wide gun is a point-blank weapon. It hits as hard as its face allows but reaches almost nowhere, which suits doorways, docking ports and boarding.

Ammo and heat follow damage. Each shot uses ammo and heats its gun in proportion to its damage (§14.7). A short gun has little mass to take that heat, so it fires hard and then waits to cool; a long gun keeps firing for longer.

A bullet stops at the part it hits. Damage a shot doesn't need to destroy that part is lost, so a hard-hitting gun is wasted on thin plating. Carrying on through is the railgun's job (below).

21.2 Railgun

The railgun is the armor-piercer. Its slug carries its remaining strength through every part it destroys, by the same rule as an explosion breaking through (§21.3) but along a single line. A strong enough shot cuts through a hull and everything lined up behind it; a part it doesn't destroy stops it. Explosions are stopped by armor, and the railgun is the answer to armor.

Stored charge sets the shot. A railgun charges from its power network (§15), and the charge it holds when fired sets the shot's strength. Its length sets how much of that charge reaches the slug, so a longer railgun hits harder from the same charge. A railgun build is a choice of power supply, rail length and cooling.

Heat limits how often it fires. Charging and firing heat it like any weapon (§14.7). Damage doesn't empty its charge.

21.3 Explosions

Armor shields what's behind it. An explosion damages each part in its radius that it reaches in a straight line from its center. A part hidden behind another takes nothing from the blast until the part in front is destroyed.

Every exposed part takes the full blast. Damage stays near full through most of the radius and drops away toward its edge: a part at distance d from a blast of radius r takes 1 − (d ÷ r)² of the blast's damage, which is three quarters at half the radius and about a third at four fifths. Each exposed part takes the blast's damage at its own distance. There is no shared pool divided among the parts hit.

What a destroyed part doesn't absorb carries on. When a part is destroyed, the damage it didn't need carries through to the parts it was shielding, still falling off with their distance. Each part uses up only as much of the blast as its own health, so a stack of thin plates stops no more than the health it adds, and a part with no health left is the same as no part at all.

Resistance makes a part cost the blast more. A part with explosion resistance, from a randmat (§9.3) or its material, as with Cement (§12), takes that much less damage, and absorbs that much more of the blast before it breaks.

Terrain stops a blast, and isn't dug by it. Nothing behind solid terrain is damaged, and the terrain itself is untouched. A claimed planet's ground can't be changed by anyone outside the claim (§18.3), and a blast that dug would get around that; a base dug into rock stays worth building; and blasting ground away is the job of the terrain-only explosive randmat (§9.3).

Explosives and EnergyBombs go off when hit. Any damage sets one off, whether a shot or a blast that reaches it, and it starts its own explosion from its center once the first one has been worked out. Charges packed together set each other off, so a rack of loaded torpedoes is something to armor.

A Warhead goes off only when triggered. Its trigger signal is its fuse: through a delay wire for a timed fuse, or from a microcontroller for an impact fuse. Damage only breaks it.

What a bin holds is inert. Explosives in a bin are items, not live charges, and are destroyed with the bin like any other contents.

An explosion lands all at once. Every part it destroys disappears in the same moment, with its effects, and power and logistics networks (§15) see the whole loss in one change.
